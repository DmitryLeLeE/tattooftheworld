Japan Irezumi Full-body suits of koi, dragons and peonies, worked by hand with tebori needles. Thailand Sak Yant Sacred geometry and Khmer script, tattooed by monks and charged with protective magic. Five Thousand Years of Ink

A short chronology of marks, bans and returns.

  • c. 3300 BCÖtzi the Iceman is tattooed with 61 carbon marks over his joints — the oldest preserved tattoos on earth; the Gebelein mummies of Egypt carry the oldest figural ones.
  • c. 500 BCThe Pazyryk horsemen of the Altai are buried in permafrost with magnificent animal-style tattoos — griffins and deer still visible on their frozen skin.
  • 1769Joseph Banks writes down the Tahitian–Samoan word tatau on Cook's first voyage, and 'tattoo' enters the world's languages.
  • 1858–1913The bans: France outlaws patu tiki in the Marquesas (1858), Meiji Japan outlaws irezumi (1872) and Ainu sinuye (1871), Japan's colonial government outlaws Atayal ptasan (1913).
  • 1891Samuel O'Reilly patents the electric tattoo machine in New York, and the flash-sheet era of American traditional begins.
  • 1948Japan legalises tattooing again; the same decade, Norman 'Sailor Jerry' Collins fuses American line with Japanese composition in Honolulu.
  • 1961–1997New York City bans tattooing outright; the craft moves to back rooms until the ban falls.
  • 1980s–nowThe revivals: tā moko returns with the Māori renaissance, patu tiki is re-learned from von den Steinen's plates, Buscalan's batok passes to a new generation, and Inuit women sew kakiniit again.
