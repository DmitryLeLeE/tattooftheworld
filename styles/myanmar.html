Myanmar

Chin Face Tattoos

Chin State · Laytu, M'uun, Dai & other Chin peoples

In the hills along the Lemro river, Chin women wore their valley's signature on their faces — webs, dots and lines that told, at a glance, exactly where a woman was from.

The Chin peoples of western Myanmar — Laytu, M'uun, Dai, Yindu, Uppriu and others — tattooed women's faces for centuries, each group in its own unmistakable pattern: a full web of fine lines covering the whole face; rows of dots like seeds; bold loops on the cheeks; some groups inked the eyelids and throat. The pattern was a passport in reverse — it named your valley, your people, your dialect.

The usual legend says the practice began as protection: a Burmese king, the story goes, took the prettiest Chin girls, and mothers began tattooing their daughters to make them undesirable to raiders. Scholars doubt the tale — the same marks read locally as beauty, maturity and belonging, and girls asked for them. Both things are true of most facial tattoo traditions: a mark against outsiders, a beauty among one's own.

The work was done by specialist women with thorn needles and soot mixed with buffalo bile and plant juice, over sessions a girl endured between the ages of about twelve and sixteen. A full M'uun web could take a day of continuous work and weeks of painful healing — faces swell; some women recalled being unable to eat for days.

Burma's socialist government banned the practice in the 1960s, and Christian conversion in the hills discouraged it further; women tattooed after that are rare. The tattooed grandmothers of the Lemro valley became, in the 2010s, one of Myanmar's most photographed sights — an uneasy economy of portraiture that at least records the faces. The youngest fully tattooed women are now old, and with them the valley signatures will go.
