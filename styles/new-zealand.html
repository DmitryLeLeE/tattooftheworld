Aotearoa · New Zealand

Tā moko

tā moko · Māori

Tā moko is not decoration but identity made visible: lineage, rank and deeds carved into the skin in spirals that belong to one person only.

The Māori brought tattooing with them across Polynesia, and on Aotearoa it took a turn found almost nowhere else: the skin was not only punctured but carved. The uhi — a chisel of albatross bone — cut grooves that healed into ridges, so that a full facial moko had texture as well as line. The tattooist, the tohunga tā moko, was a tapu specialist, paid in treasures and fed by others while the work healed.

A moko is a document. The spirals and curves of the face are arranged in zones that speak of descent on the mother's and father's side, of rank, of standing within the hapū. Chiefs who signed the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840 drew their moko as their signature — the most literal proof that a face pattern was understood as a name. Women traditionally wore the moko kauae on lips and chin.

Colonisation nearly extinguished the practice: missionary pressure, musket wars and the grim trade in preserved tattooed heads (mokomokai) made moko dangerous to wear. By the early twentieth century the full male facial moko had all but disappeared, surviving mainly in the portraits of painters like Gottfried Lindauer and in the chin moko of elder women.

Since the Māori renaissance of the 1980s, tā moko has returned as a living taonga — a treasure. Artists revived the forms, and today moko kauae are worn by members of parliament and broadcasters on the national news. The distinction matters here more than anywhere: moko belongs to whakapapa, to genealogy. Māori-style patterns worn by outsiders are called kirituhi — skin art — a courtesy of separation the tradition itself asks for.
