North Africa

Amazigh Tattoos

ⵜⴰⵎⴰⵣⵉⵖⵜ · Berber women's tattoos

Across the Atlas mountains and the Maghreb plains, Amazigh women wore tattoos the way the land wears its marks: a palm on the chin, a diamond on the brow, dots at the corner of the eye.

The Imazighen — the free people, whom outsiders called Berbers — carried a tattoo tradition measured in millennia, worn almost entirely by women. The marks sat where clothing never covered: the chin, the forehead, cheeks, the backs of hands, ankles. A vertical chin line called siyala, palm fronds, diamonds, chevrons and crosses — the same vocabulary woven into carpets and painted on pottery across the region.

The marks worked. They protected against the evil eye, carried fertility and healing (a dot at the temple against headache, marks over an ailing joint), identified tribe and region, and recorded a woman's passages — betrothal, marriage, children. Applied around puberty by older women with soot and needle, they were medicine, jewellery and record in one.

Islamic orthodoxy regards tattooing as forbidden, and for centuries the practice lived in negotiation with that judgment — sometimes relaxed, sometimes enforced. Through the twentieth century, urbanisation, schooling and religious revival steadily ended new tattoos; many older women were even persuaded to regret theirs. The last fully tattooed generation — women now in their eighties and nineties in the Atlas and Aurès — are the tradition's final primary sources.

Documentation has become the afterlife of the practice: photographers and granddaughters recording faces while they remain, and a young Amazigh diaspora re-wearing the symbols — sometimes in ink, more often in silver — as a statement of identity in a region still arguing about its Amazigh roots.
