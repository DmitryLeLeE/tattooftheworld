Samoa

Tatau

pe'a (men) · malu (women)

Every tattoo on earth carries a Samoan word. Tatau — the sound of the mallet on the comb — became 'tattoo' when Cook's crews brought it home in 1769.

Samoa is the tradition that never broke. While missionaries suppressed tattooing across most of the Pacific, the Samoan tufuga tā tatau — master tattooists of hereditary guilds like the Sulu'ape family — kept working through the colonial era to the present day, with the same tools: a comb of bone or boar tusk lashed to a turtle-shell plate, struck with a mallet, and ink traditionally made from burnt candlenut soot.

The men's tattoo, the pe'a, runs from mid-torso to the knees — dense fields of straight black lines, zones and canoe motifs, laid out by eye without a stencil. It is finished over days or weeks of sessions attended by family, and to abandon it halfway, remaining pe'a mutu, is a lasting shame. The women's malu is lighter: an open lacework on the thighs, named for its central diamond motif of shelter and protection.

Receiving the tatau is a social act. It marks readiness to serve the family and village — to make the kava, to speak, to sit among the chiefs. The pain is the point as much as the pattern: borne quietly, with a relative holding the skin taut, it is proof of the endurance the design announces.

From Samoa and its neighbours the lines radiate outward — to Tonga, Tahiti, the Marquesas whose dense geometry astonished the first Europeans, and Hawai'i. The modern 'tribal' style of the 1990s is a distant, simplified echo of this family of patterns; the originals remain precise legal-social documents, applied by hand to the sound the word still remembers.
