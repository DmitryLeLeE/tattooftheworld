United States

American Traditional

old school · sailor tattoo

Thick black outlines, five flat colours, and a promise in the trade's own words: bold will hold. The flash sheet on the parlor wall is this style's scripture — and this site's design.

American traditional grew in port towns and navy yards. Sailors had carried tattoo customs home since the Pacific voyages of the 1700s, and by the Civil War era professional artists worked the waterfronts. In 1891 Samuel O'Reilly of New York patented the first electric tattoo machine, built on Edison's autographic pen — and speed changed everything. Designs standardised into flash: sheets of ready-to-wear images pinned to the wall, picked by number.

The iconography is a sailor's diary. A swallow for 5,000 nautical miles — and for coming home, as swallows do. An anchor for stability or an Atlantic crossing; a full-rigged ship for rounding Cape Horn; HOLD FAST on the knuckles to keep grip on the rigging; a pig and a rooster on the feet against drowning. Roses, daggers, hearts marked MOM — love and luck in five pigments that would survive decades of sun and salt.

The style's grammar was set by working artists with navy clientele: heavy outline laid first, black shading, then red, green, yellow, brown. No gradients a healed tattoo couldn't keep. Norman 'Sailor Jerry' Collins, working from Honolulu's Hotel Street through the Second World War, fused the American line with Japanese composition he admired across the Pacific, and trained the generation — Ed Hardy among them — that carried the style into the tattoo renaissance.

Driven near-underground by mid-century bans (New York City outlawed tattooing outright from 1961 to 1997), old school returned as the backbone of the modern craft. Its rules still teach apprentices what a tattoo must do: read across a room, and hold for a lifetime.
