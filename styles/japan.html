Japan

Irezumi

入れ墨 · 和彫り (wabori)

In Japan the tattoo became a garment: a single composition of water, wind, flowers and legend, cut to the shape of a jacket and worn for life.

Japanese tattooing is ancient — clay figures with incised faces go back millennia — but irezumi as we know it flowered in the Edo period (1603–1868). Woodblock prints of the Suikoden, a Chinese epic of outlaw heroes, swept the cities in the early 1800s; its heroes were drawn covered in tattoos, and firemen, labourers and palanquin bearers wanted the same skin. The print masters and the tattoo masters shared motifs, composition and even names: a tattooist is a horishi — a carver.

A classical body suit is one picture, not a collection. A background of wind bars and waves ties together koi carp (perseverance), dragons (wisdom and power), tigers, peonies, cherry blossom and figures from legend. The suit keeps a strict border — stopping at the wrists, the collar and above the knee — so that a kimono, or a rolled sleeve, hides it completely. Concealment is part of the form.

Tradition works by hand. In tebori, the master drives a rake of needles bound to a bamboo or steel handle with a steady rock of the wrist. The ink is sumi, the same carbon black used for calligraphy, which heals into a blue-grey that machine black rarely matches. A back piece can take years of weekly sittings, and the client traditionally earns the final signature panel only on completion.

The Meiji government banned tattooing in 1872 as the country opened to the West, and the craft survived underground — which welded it, in the public mind, to the yakuza. The ban fell in 1948, but many bathhouses and hot springs still turn tattooed guests away. Meanwhile the style itself has become one of the most imitated visual languages in world tattooing: every "Japanese sleeve" done in London or São Paulo is a quotation of Edo.
