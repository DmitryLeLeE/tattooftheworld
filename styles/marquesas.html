Marquesas Islands

Enata Patterns

patu tiki · te patutiki

When Europeans first anchored off the Marquesas, they met men tattooed over every inch of skin, eyelids included — the most complete body writing any culture has produced.

The Marquesas — Te Henua 'Enana, the Land of Men — pushed Polynesian tattooing to its absolute limit. Where Samoa stopped at the knees, the Marquesan ideal was total coverage: a chief in his sixties might be blue-black from scalp to sole, the designs layered over decades until the earliest work disappeared under the latest. The practice is called patu tiki — 'striking images'.

The vocabulary is geometric and dense: etua figures of gods and ancestors, ipu containers, checkerboards, eye motifs (mata) that watch in all directions, and the stylised faces of tiki. Each motif is a word; a covered body is a biography, a genealogy and a shield of mana at once. Russian and French expedition artists of the early 1800s — Tilesius, Radiguet — drew the warriors of Nuku Hiva in astonished detail, and those plates remain the style's best record.

Tattooing here was wrapped in tapu: sessions in special houses, feasting obligations on the family, first tattoos marking a young man's entry into the warrior age-grade. Women wore finer work — hands, lips, ears, shoulders — gloves of pattern that marked rank and the right to prepare food for the tapu class.

French colonial law banned tattooing in 1858, and missionary pressure had nearly ended it already; within two generations full-body patu tiki was gone. Its revival since the 1980s runs on the old plates: the German ethnographer Karl von den Steinen's three-volume record (1925) became the unlikely pattern book from which Marquesan artists re-learned their own grammar. Today patu tiki again marks Marquesan identity across French Polynesia — and the 2024 inscription of the islands on the UNESCO World Heritage list cited the living art among their treasures.
