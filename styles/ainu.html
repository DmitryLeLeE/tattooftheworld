Hokkaidō · Japan

Ainu Sinuye

シヌイェ · mouth tattoo (anchi-piri)

Among the Ainu of Hokkaidō, Sakhalin and the Kurils, a woman's tattoo began in childhood as a dot on the lip and grew, year by year, into the broad smile-shaped mark of adulthood.

The Ainu, the indigenous people of Japan's far north, kept a tattoo tradition entirely of women, made by women. The sinuye began with a small mark on a girl's upper lip, enlarged over years until, by marriageable age, a full tattoo framed the mouth — with matching bands and interlace on the forearms and hands. The pigment was birch-bark soot, cut into the skin and wiped with an antiseptic brew of ash bark.

The mouth tattoo made a woman complete: ready for marriage, protected against illness-spirits entering by the lips, and recognisable to her ancestors in the world after death. Untattooed, a woman risked going unrecognised. The tattooist was an aunt or grandmother, and the craft passed down the female line with its chants and taboos.

The Japanese state outlawed Ainu tattooing twice — in 1799 and again in 1871 — as part of assimilation policy that also banned the language and earrings of men. Women kept tattooing in secret for decades; the last women with full traditional sinuye died in the late twentieth century. Photographs from the Meiji era to the 1950s preserve the form the law tried to erase.

Today, as Ainu identity is publicly reclaimed — the people were recognised as indigenous by Japan only in 2008 and by law in 2019 — the tattoo returns mostly as image: drawn on for dance performances and festivals, studied by descendants, and occasionally re-inked for good by Ainu women taking the old mark into a new century.
