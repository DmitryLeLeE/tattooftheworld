Philippines

Batok

batok · batek · Kalinga

In the Cordillera mountains of Luzon, tattoos were earned: centipedes and eagles for warriors, ladders and rice bundles for abundance, tapped into the skin with a pomelo thorn.

When Spanish ships reached the Visayas in the 1500s they called the islands Las Islas de los Pintados — the isles of the painted ones — because the people they met were tattooed from head to foot. Colonisation and conversion erased most of that lowland tradition; it survived in the mountains of northern Luzon, among peoples the empire never fully reached — Kalinga, Bontoc, Ifugao.

Batok is tapped, not poked: a thorn from a pomelo or calamansi tree set in a short stick, struck with a second stick, driving soot from cooking fires under the skin. The patterns are geometric and local — gayaman, the centipede guide of warriors; the python's skin; ladders, rice bundles, and the stepped chest pieces that marked a Kalinga man who had taken heads in defence of his village. For women, arm tattoos marked beauty, fertility and standing.

The tradition narrowed to a single village and very nearly to a single pair of hands. Apo Whang-od Oggay of Buscalan, born around 1917, learned batok as a teenager and kept practising when every other mambabatok had stopped. Rediscovered by documentary-makers in the 2000s, she became a national icon — a Vogue cover at 106 — and her village a pilgrimage: travellers hike the rice terraces to receive her three-dot signature.

Because the craft passes only within the bloodline, Whang-od trained her grandnieces Grace Palicas and Elyang Wigan, and dozens of young Buscalan tattooists now work the thorn. The revival runs on tourism, with everything that brings — but the sound in the village is the old one: tap, tap, tap.
