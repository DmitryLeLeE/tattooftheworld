Taiwan

Atayal Ptasan

ptasan · 紋面 · Atayal, Seediq, Truku

Among the Atayal of Taiwan's mountains, the face tattoo was a diploma: no man received his without proving courage, no woman hers without mastering the loom — and without it, no marriage.

The Atayal, Seediq and Truku peoples of Taiwan's central mountains practised ptasan, facial tattooing, as the mark of full adulthood. A boy earned the vertical chin stripe traditionally by taking an enemy head or otherwise proving himself in defence of the community; a girl earned the broad cheek bands — sweeping from the ears to the lips — by weaving cloth fine enough to satisfy her elders. Both sexes received a forehead mark in childhood.

The marks were a visa for the afterlife. Ancestral spirits waiting at the rainbow bridge, hongu utux, examined the faces of the dead: the properly tattooed crossed to the ancestors, the unmarked could not. A face without ptasan was not just unmarriageable — it was unrecognisable to one's own dead.

Tattooists were women, paid specialists who tapped soot into the skin with needle combs and a striking stick — the design pricked deep enough that a full set of women's cheek bands took a day and healed over a month, during which the girl ate through a wooden mouthpiece.

The Japanese colonial government outlawed ptasan in 1913 as part of its campaign against indigenous custom (it banned the headhunting that fed the men's tattoo in the same stroke), and enforced the ban with fines and forced removal. The last tattooed generation grew old through the twentieth century; the final fully tattooed Atayal elders died in the 2010s. Today the patterns live on in revival ceremonies, museum work by descendants, and — a familiar story across this atlas — in young people re-inking the old lines by choice.
